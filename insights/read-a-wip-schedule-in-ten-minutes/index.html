How to Read a WIP Schedule in Ten Minutes

The work-in-progress schedule is the single most honest document in a construction company. Here is the owner's ten-minute review: five numbers, four questions, and the warning signs that matter.

By Jake Schreiber, Founder, 1LINE · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

If a contractor could keep only one financial report, it should be the work-in-progress (WIP) schedule. The income statement tells you what the accounting system concluded. The WIP tells you why — job by job, while there is still time to do something about it.

You do not need to audit it. You need a ten-minute owner's review, in a fixed order, every month.

The five numbers, in order

1. Contract value, including approved change orders. If unapproved change orders are inflating this number, everything downstream is fiction. 2. Costs incurred to date. 3. Estimated cost to complete — the number that requires judgment, and therefore the number to interrogate. 4. Percent complete (costs to date divided by total estimated cost). 5. Earned revenue, billings to date, and the difference between them: overbilling or underbilling.

Read down those columns job by job, then read the totals. A company-level profit figure built on forty jobs is only as honest as its least honest estimate.

Four questions that find the problems

Where did the margin fade? Compare each job's current estimated gross margin with the margin it was bid at. Fade concentrated in late-stage jobs usually means the estimate-to-complete is optimistic; fade everywhere usually means estimating or labor productivity has a real problem.

Which jobs are underbilled, and why? Underbilling with a story — a pending change order, a billing dispute — is a cash issue. Underbilling without a story is often an earnings issue wearing a disguise.

Which jobs are heavily overbilled? Overbilling is borrowed cash from the customer. It is normal within limits, but a job 90% billed at 60% complete is financing today's payroll with tomorrow's performance — and the bill comes due at the end of the job.

What changed since last month? A WIP review is a comparison, not a portrait. Estimates that move every month in the favorable direction early and collapse at completion are the classic pattern behind the year-end "surprise" loss.

The warning signs

Treat these as stop-and-ask signals: a job whose estimated cost to complete has not changed in two months despite ongoing work; percent complete rising while billings stall; gross margin on completed jobs consistently lower than the margin those same jobs showed at 80% complete (the "fade at the finish" pattern); and any job where the project manager and the accounting system tell different stories about the same number.

Ten minutes, monthly, in a fixed order. The WIP will not fix a job for you — but it will never let a bad job surprise you twice.
